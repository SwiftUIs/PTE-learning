In today’s competitive education landscape, formal written examinations remain one of the most common methods of assessing students’ knowledge and abilities. While some critics argue that exams create unnecessary pressure and fail to measure true learning, I largely agree that formal written examinations are a valid and effective assessment method.First and foremost, written exams provide a standardized and objective way to evaluate large numbers of students simultaneously. Unlike continuous assessment or projects, which can be influenced by subjective marking or external help, formal examinations ensure fairness and consistency across different schools and regions. For example, in countries like China, Japan, and South Korea, national exams such as the Gaokao or university entrance tests have successfully identified talented students and promoted social mobility for decades.Moreover, the preparation process for written exams encourages students to review and consolidate their knowledge systematically. This discipline not only deepens understanding but also develops important skills such as time management, critical thinking, and stress handling — all of which are valuable in higher education and future careers.Admittedly, formal examinations have limitations. They tend to reward rote memorization rather than creativity or practical skills, and high-stakes testing can cause severe anxiety for some students. Alternative forms of assessment, such as coursework, presentations, and group projects, can complement exams by evaluating a broader range of abilities.In conclusion, despite their drawbacks, I strongly believe that formal written examinations are still a valid and necessary method of assessment in most education systems. They offer reliability and standardization that other methods often struggle to match. A balanced approach that combines exams with continuous assessment would probably be ideal, but completely replacing written exams would be unwise.
